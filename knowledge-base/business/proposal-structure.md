# Fractional COO Proposal Structure

## Proposal Principles

A proposal is a diagnostic summary + a prescription + a business case. It is not a capabilities deck. The worst proposals spend three pages on the consultant's background and one paragraph on the client's specific problem. Invert this.

Rule: Every section of the proposal should be grounded in what was heard and observed during the diagnostic — not generic consulting language.

**Length:** 4–6 pages. Any longer and it won't be read fully. Any shorter and it lacks credibility.
**Format:** PDF. Delivered by email, reviewed on a 30-minute call. Never drop it and walk away — always walk through it live.

---

## Section 1: Executive Summary (1/2 page)

**Purpose:** Busy owner reads this first. If the executive summary is sharp, they'll read the rest. If it's vague, they'll skim.

**What to include:**
- Two to three sentences summarizing what you heard: the owner's situation, growth stage, and primary pain
- One sentence on what the diagnostic revealed
- One sentence on what you're recommending and the expected outcome

**Tone:** Confident, specific, empathetic. Not clinical. Not salesy.

**Example opening:**
"After our diagnostic session, it's clear that [Company Name] has built a strong revenue base but is operating without the infrastructure to sustain the growth ahead. The most urgent gaps are in job costing visibility, cash flow forecasting, and scheduling accountability. This proposal outlines a 6-month engagement to install the operating systems that will allow [Owner Name] to step back from daily firefighting and grow with confidence."

---

## Section 2: Problem Diagnosis (1–1.5 pages)

**Purpose:** Show that you understood the problem — not just the symptoms they described, but the underlying structural issues.

**Format:** 3–5 identified problems, each with:
- A name (headline for the problem)
- One to two sentences describing what you observed or what the data showed
- The business impact (quantified where possible)

**Example:**
**Problem: Gross Margin Erosion Without Visibility**
"Your P&L shows gross margin at 34% for the trailing 12 months, down from 41% two years ago. However, the company has no job costing system — margin is measured at the company level, not the job level. Without job-level visibility, it's impossible to know which service lines, crews, or job types are profitable and which are eroding the blended margin."

**Impact:** At $6M revenue, a 7-point margin gap represents approximately $420,000 in potential gross profit not being captured.

---

## Section 3: Proposed Scope (1–1.5 pages)

**Purpose:** Define exactly what Framework OPS will do — no ambiguity.

**Format:** Organize by phase or workstream. Each item should be a named deliverable, not a vague activity.

**Good:** "Design and implement a 13-week rolling cash flow model in Google Sheets, train office manager on weekly update process, establish Monday morning review cadence — delivered by Day 45."

**Bad:** "Improve cash flow management and financial visibility."

**Phases (typical for a 6-month engagement):**
- **Phase 1 (Days 1–30): Diagnostic and Infrastructure**
  - Complete operational diagnostic (P&L, FSM audit, org assessment)
  - Establish KPI dashboard (8–12 metrics, reviewed weekly)
  - Cash flow model built and trained

- **Phase 2 (Days 31–60): Quick Wins and Systems Install**
  - Job costing framework implemented in FSM
  - Top 3 SOPs documented (dispatch, job closeout, estimate process)
  - Lead follow-up automation built

- **Phase 3 (Days 61–180): Scaling Infrastructure**
  - Hiring process standardized (job description, scorecard, interview guide)
  - Manager accountability cadence established
  - Monthly P&L review process installed
  - 90-day business review facilitated

**What is NOT included:** Be explicit. This prevents scope creep and manages expectations.

---

## Section 4: Engagement Structure and Hours (1/2 page)

- Engagement tier (Advisory / Operational Partner / Embedded)
- Monthly hours
- Meeting cadence (weekly session, async support)
- Communication channels
- Client-side time commitment (what you need from them)

---

## Section 5: Timeline (1/2 page)

A visual or simple table works here. Show:
- Key milestones with target dates
- Decision points (30-day review, 90-day review, renewal)
- Dependencies: "Cash flow model requires QBO access and last 12 months of P&L by Day 5"

---

## Section 6: Investment (1/4 page)

State the number clearly. Do not bury it or surround it with qualifiers.

**Format:**
- Monthly retainer: $X/month
- Initial commitment: X months
- Payment terms: First month due on signing; monthly thereafter on [date]
- Optional: what happens after the initial term (month-to-month with 30-day notice)

Do not present three tiers in the proposal — that's a scope conversation you should have had before writing the proposal. Presenting tiers at proposal stage signals you don't know what they need.

---

## Section 7: ROI Framing (1/2 page)

**Purpose:** Give the owner a rational business case to justify the investment internally (often to a spouse, business partner, or themselves).

**Do not be aggressive or promises-based.** Frame conservatively and let the math speak.

**Example:**
"At $4,500/month, this engagement costs $54,000 over 12 months. Based on our diagnostic, there are three identified value creation opportunities:

1. **Gross margin recovery:** Improving from 34% to 38% on $6M in revenue = $240,000 additional gross profit annually
2. **Cash flow management:** Avoiding one unplanned LOC draw of $75,000 at 8% interest = $6,000/year in interest savings
3. **Owner time recapture:** Reducing owner hours from 65 to 50 per week through delegation = immeasurable quality of life, plus allows focus on higher-value sales activity

Conservative value creation estimate: $200,000–$300,000 in Year 1 if fully implemented. The engagement pays for itself if even one of these outcomes is achieved."

**Caveat:** Explicitly note that outcomes depend on client-side implementation. Framework OPS builds the systems; the client team executes them.

---

## Section 8: Next Steps (1/4 page)

One clear, time-bound action:

"To move forward: sign the agreement and submit the first month's retainer by [specific date, typically 7 days out]. I'll send the kickoff scheduling link within 24 hours of receiving both. This proposal expires on [date]."

If they don't take action by the deadline, a short follow-up call is appropriate. After that, move on. Clients who won't make a decision don't implement, either.

---

## Proposal Mistakes to Avoid

- **No diagnostic before proposal.** If you're writing proposals without completing a diagnostic, you're guessing at the problem and the scope will be wrong.
- **Starting with your bio.** The client wants to know you understand their business, not read your CV.
- **Vague deliverables.** "Improve operations" is not a deliverable. Every scope item must be specific.
- **Missing the "so what" on the problem.** Naming a problem is not enough — quantify the cost of the problem wherever possible.
- **No clear next step.** A proposal without a specific call to action will sit in an inbox and age.
