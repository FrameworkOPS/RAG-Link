# Cash Flow Management for Home Service Operators

## Why Cash Flow Is Not the Same as Profit

A roofing company can be profitable on paper and run out of cash in June. This happens constantly in home service. The gap between recognized revenue and cash in the bank is created by: timing of payments (deposits, draws, finals), material float (paying for shingles before the job is invoiced), payroll frequency, and seasonal swings.

The owner who checks the bank balance to understand financial health is operating blind. The owner who runs a 13-week rolling cash flow model is operating the business.

---

## The 13-Week Rolling Cash Flow Model

The single most impactful financial tool for a home service operator. This is not complicated — it's a spreadsheet that answers one question: "What will my cash balance be 13 weeks from now if everything happens as expected?"

### Structure

| Column | Content |
|---|---|
| Week 1–13 | Date of each week |
| Opening Cash Balance | Cash in bank at start of week |
| Cash Inflows | Expected payments to arrive (by customer/job) |
| Cash Outflows | Expected payments out (payroll, materials, overhead, taxes) |
| Net Cash Flow | Inflows - Outflows |
| Closing Cash Balance | Running total |

### Inputs Required
- Outstanding AR with expected payment dates (pulled from FSM or QBO)
- Upcoming payroll dates and amounts
- Material orders placed or upcoming (accounts payable)
- Fixed overhead costs (insurance, rent, software, truck payments)
- Any large one-time outflows (tax payments, equipment purchases, loan payments)

### How to Use It
- Update weekly, every Monday morning before the day starts
- Flag any week where projected closing balance drops below the minimum safe cash buffer (see below)
- If a week shows a projected shortfall, you have time to act: accelerate a collection, delay a purchase, draw on the line of credit proactively

**Minimum safe cash buffer:** 4–6 weeks of total overhead + payroll. For a company with $80K/month overhead and $60K/month payroll, the minimum buffer is $280K–$420K. Most home service operators run too lean on this.

## Draw Schedules and Progress Billing

For roofing, construction, and larger-ticket home service work, never bill entirely at the end. Use a draw schedule.

### Standard Residential Roofing Draw Structure
- **Deposit at signing:** 30–50% of contract value
- **Material delivery:** optional second draw when materials are delivered (10–20%)
- **Job completion:** remaining balance due on completion day or within 7 days
- **Insurance jobs exception:** first check often comes at ACV (actual cash value) before supplement, with final payment after supplement approval. Educate clients on this timeline upfront.

### Commercial Draw Structure
- Negotiate monthly progress billing for any job over 30 days
- Net 30 terms are standard in commercial, but push for net 15 where possible
- Require a signed contract with explicit payment milestones before any work starts

### Why Deposits Matter for Cash Flow
A roofing company doing $500K/month in revenue with 40% deposits collects $200K upfront, covering most material costs before labor starts. Without deposits, the company is floating $100K–$200K in material costs at any given time — a major cash drain during growth.

## Material Float Management

**Material float** is the time between when you pay for materials and when you collect payment for the job. It's a hidden cash flow killer during growth phases.

### Strategies to Minimize Float
1. **Use supplier terms.** Net 30 from major distributors (ABC Supply, Beacon, SRS for roofing) gives you time to collect the job before paying for materials. Negotiate these terms aggressively — they're standard for established accounts.
2. **Tie material orders to job deposits.** Do not order materials without a deposit in hand. The deposit covers materials; the final payment covers labor and margin.
3. **Track open POs against scheduled work.** Know your total material exposure at any moment.
4. **Limit speculative inventory.** Buying materials ahead "because the price is good" ties up cash. Only buy ahead if you have firm backlog to consume it.

## Payroll Timing

Payroll is typically the largest single cash outflow in a service company and the least flexible. Payroll must happen on time regardless of AR collection issues.

### Best Practices
- **Bi-weekly payroll** is most common and manageable. Avoid weekly payroll for field staff — it increases administrative burden and creates more cash flow pressure.
- **Set payroll dates on your 13-week model.** Know when every payroll hits and make sure the projected balance covers it with $50K+ buffer.
- **Commission and bonus payroll:** Pay separately from base wages, and only after the job revenue has been collected. Paying commissions on work that hasn't been paid for accelerates the cash flow problem.
- **Avoid payroll surprises:** Seasonal ramp-up (adding 5–8 field workers in spring) can increase monthly payroll by $30K–$60K virtually overnight. Model this out 8–10 weeks in advance.

## Line of Credit (LOC) Strategy

A business line of credit is a tool, not a lifeline. Using it reactively (drawing on it because the bank balance is suddenly low) means you've already failed at cash flow management.

### How to Use a LOC Correctly
1. **Draw proactively** when the 13-week model shows a projected shortfall 4–6 weeks out
2. **Repay as soon as cash comes in** — do not let the LOC balance sit outstanding
3. **Never use the LOC for long-term asset purchases** (trucks, equipment) — that's what equipment financing is for
4. **Size the LOC to your peak working capital need,** not your average. A company with $6M in revenue and $400K peak material float needs at minimum a $250K–$400K LOC, not $100K.

### How to Qualify for a Business LOC (Mercury, Live Oak, SBA)
- 2+ years in business with tax returns showing profitability
- QBO/books that are clean and accurate (this is a significant underwriting factor)
- Personal guarantee typically required under $500K
- Revenue and banking history (Mercury is strong for this — use it consistently)

**Do not wait until you need the LOC to apply for it.** Apply when the business is healthy. Banks extend credit to companies that don't need it, not to companies that desperately do.

## Common Cash Flow Mistakes in Home Service

**1. Confusing revenue with cash.** $6M in revenue doesn't mean $500K in the bank. Revenue is an accounting entry. Cash is what runs payroll.

**2. Not separating operating cash from tax reserves.** Set aside 25–30% of net profit in a separate account for quarterly estimated taxes. Getting hit with a $60K tax bill in April after a good year will devastate cash flow.

**3. Seasonal staffing ramp without cash reserves.** Hiring 8 techs in March when bank account is at $80K. Cash break-even on new hires is typically 60–90 days. Model it out.

**4. Slow collections on commercial work.** Commercial customers pay on their terms unless pushed. Assign AR aging reviews weekly. Invoice immediately at job completion. Charge late fees (and enforce them).

**5. Overinvesting in trucks/equipment for growth.** A $50K truck purchased with cash instead of financed is a $50K cash flow event. Finance long-lived assets. Preserve cash for operations.
